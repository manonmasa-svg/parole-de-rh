> **In brief:**
> 1. For Franck PRIME, author of this op-ed, eight **transferable skills** gained in the events industry are underestimated by recruiters: project management, negotiation, cross-functional management, crisis management, digital marketing, budget control, relational intelligence and adaptability.
> 2. He recommends judging a candidate on measurable achievements rather than on their last job title, and checking their skills through work simulations.
> 3. According to France Travail, 2.2 million hires are planned in France in 2026 and nearly one job in two (43.8%) remains without an ideal candidate.
> 4. France Travail also notes that between 2016 and 2021, one in four workers undertook a career change.

What if events professionals held some of the skills companies look for most, without recruiters always knowing how to spot them? Behind the organisation of a trade show or a conference lies know-how in management, negotiation, crisis management, digital marketing and financial control. For HR departments, this experience is a talent pool that deserves better recognition.

## Events, an often overlooked school of management

A trade show is more than stands, talks and visitors. It is a temporary business, with its own commercial goals, teams, suppliers, financial constraints and need for results.

Unlike many activities where projects can be extended or reorganised, an event has one particular feature: its opening date cannot be negotiated.

At the scheduled time, everything must work.

This requirement deeply changes the way people work. It forces them to anticipate, decide, negotiate, communicate and solve problems within sometimes extremely short timeframes.

Over more than thirty years as an entrepreneur in the events industry, I have taken part in creating and developing many trade shows in France and abroad, particularly in human resources, digital marketing, e-commerce and luxury.

According to my business records, the projects I contributed to represent around 350 organisations in 15 countries and some four million visitors in total.

This experience has led me to one conviction: skills gained in the events industry are largely transferable to other sectors, but companies still do not recognise their value enough.

Here are eight skills of particular interest to recruiters.

## 1. Project management: turning an idea into an operational result

Organising an event means building a complete project from a goal.

You have to define a positioning, draw up a budget, set a schedule, select suppliers, sell an offer, mobilise teams and measure results.

Each step depends on the previous ones.

A delay in sales can jeopardise revenue. A logistics error can affect the participants' experience. Insufficient communication can reduce attendance.

Events professionals therefore learn to manage several parameters at once, sometimes conflicting ones.

For an HR director, this experience can be relevant for roles such as project manager, operations manager, programme coordinator or production director.

It must however be assessed on the candidate's actual achievements: budget managed, autonomy, number of contributors coordinated, deadlines met and results obtained.

## 2. Commercial negotiation: convincing in a competitive environment

A trade show organiser often has to convince several audiences: exhibitors, sponsors, institutional partners, service providers and business visitors.

They are not just selling floor space. They are defending a value proposition, potential business contacts and growth prospects.

This activity requires understanding a company's needs, identifying its objections, defending a financial proposal and building a lasting relationship.

In my experience developing trade shows, particularly in digital marketing and human resources, the ability to bring partners and companies on board at the same time has always been essential.

This skill transfers to B2B business development, strategic partnerships, key account management or purchasing.

The recruiter should look for evidence: portfolio grown, contracts negotiated, retention, partner renewals and contribution to revenue.

## 3. Cross-functional management: leading without always having formal authority

An event brings together teams whose members rarely belong to a single organisation.

Salespeople, technicians, audiovisual providers, communication staff, hosts, security managers, suppliers and speakers must work together.

The events manager does not necessarily have line authority over each of them.

Yet they must secure their cooperation.

This situation develops a fundamental skill: cross-functional management.

It involves clarifying responsibilities, explaining priorities, preventing conflicts and making sure commitments are kept.

This experience is particularly valuable for matrix organisations, multi-site companies or organisations undergoing transformation.

An HR director can assess it by asking the candidate to describe a situation in which they had to coordinate several independent teams to reach a shared goal.

## 4. Crisis management: deciding when time is short

In the events industry, an incident can occur minutes before opening.

A speaker does not show up. A supplier has a technical problem. A delivery is late. A last-minute change affects the programme.

The question is no longer whether the organisation was perfect on paper, but whether it can work despite the unexpected.

An experienced professional learns to rank risks, separate the essential from the secondary, share out responsibilities and make a quick decision.

This ability does not mean improvising without method.

On the contrary, the best managers prepare backup scenarios, set up procedures and anticipate points of failure.

This know-how is of particular interest to industrial companies, customer service departments, logistics organisations and operations departments.

The real skill is not working permanently in emergency mode: it is reducing the impact of emergencies through anticipation.

## 5. Digital marketing and data management: understanding and engaging audiences

The success of a trade show also depends on its ability to identify, attract and retain a relevant audience.

This involves segmenting audiences, managing contact databases, building campaigns, producing content and analysing results.

At trade shows devoted to digital marketing, e-commerce or human resources, this dimension is especially important.

Events professionals can thus gain skills in CRM, email marketing, lead generation, data analysis and multichannel communication.

But one distinction remains essential: using a tool does not necessarily mean mastering it.

For a recruiter, it is better to ask the candidate to explain a specific campaign: its goal, the targeting chosen, the tools used and the results actually measured.

These experiences can be a bridge to operational marketing, communication, business development or customer relations.

## 6. Budget control: understanding the financial impact of every decision

A business event requires investment before it generates any of its revenue.

Venue hire, communication, fit-out, equipment, technical services and human resources are all cost items to control.

The manager must constantly balance quality, costs and profitability.

They learn to build forecasts, negotiate expenses, track commitments and measure the gap between targets and actual results.

This practice is hands-on financial training.

In my business activities, I have seen how much financial control could determine whether an event survives, regardless of its attendance or prestige.

For HR directors, this experience deserves particular attention when recruiting profit centre managers, operations directors or business unit managers.

A serious assessment must nonetheless distinguish simply taking part in a budget from being truly responsible for building and steering it.

## 7. Relational intelligence: understanding very different people

A trade show brings together people whose expectations sometimes conflict.

The exhibitor wants business opportunities. The visitor wants useful information. The partner expects visibility. The speaker wants to share their expertise. The organiser has to reconcile all of these.

This diversity calls for strong listening skills.

An experienced professional learns to adapt their message, spot tensions, handle objections and maintain relationships over time.

This skill is particularly valuable in jobs that involve regular interaction with clients, partners, colleagues or institutions.

It can contribute to success in sales, management and relationship roles.

However, being a good speaker is not enough to show relational intelligence. Recruiters must also observe the ability to listen, rephrase, negotiate and resolve a disagreement.

## 8. Adaptability and learning ability: evolving with markets

The events industry has undergone major changes: online registration, hybrid events, new marketing practices, new environmental requirements, automation and artificial intelligence tools.

A professional who has lived through these changes has necessarily had to question their methods.

That does not guarantee they master every recent technology. But their career path can reveal a real ability to learn, experiment and adapt an organisation.

The central question for HR directors is therefore not only: "What have you done?"

It becomes: "What have you learned, what have you changed and how could you apply this experience with us?"

This ability to evolve is decisive in supporting company transformations.

## How can HR directors identify these skills?

The first mistake is to reduce a candidate to their last job title.

A trade show manager may have carried out tasks close to those of an operations director. An events partnerships officer may have built solid B2B negotiation experience. A conference coordinator may have managed complex teams.

These matches must nevertheless be checked.

I recommend a simple four-step approach.

First, look at achievements rather than job titles. Which projects did the candidate actually lead? With what responsibilities?

Second, look for measurable evidence. Budgets, deadlines, business volumes, sales results or the number of people coordinated give a better picture of the experience.

Third, use work simulations. Preparing a backward schedule, solving an operational incident or presenting a negotiation lets you observe concrete skills.

Fourth, identify the additional skills to acquire. Transferable experience does not always replace technical, regulatory or sector-specific expertise. Suitable training can ease the transition.

This approach is in line with recruitment methods based on skills and abilities, including those developed by France Travail.

## Training, a bridge between experience and new jobs

Recognising transferable skills does not mean assuming a professional can immediately do any job.

Career mobility sometimes requires new learning.

An events salesperson who wants to become a marketing manager may need to strengthen their data analysis knowledge. A logistics manager aiming for an operations director role may need to deepen their management control skills. A coordinator who wants to move into human resources may need training in employment law or skills management.

The challenge is to avoid systematically starting a full training course from scratch when the candidate already has a large share of the expected skills.

Short courses, work-based simulations and learning directly on the job can provide suitable answers to these needs.

Provided they start from a serious assessment of existing skills and missing ones.

## An opportunity for companies facing recruitment difficulties

Companies often look for profiles that are immediately operational, able to work in a team, adapt and deliver results.

Yet their selection criteria can sometimes rule out candidates who developed these abilities in another sector.

The events industry illustrates this contradiction particularly well.

Its professionals work in demanding commercial, human and technical environments. They work to fixed deadlines, numerical targets and with many stakeholders.

Their experience can be an asset in many organisations, without removing the need to check the abilities each role requires.

For HR departments, the opportunity is twofold: widening talent pools and supporting career mobility more effectively.

After more than thirty years devoted to creating, developing and organising business events, I remain convinced of one thing:

A career should not be judged only by the sector in which it was built, but by the skills demonstrated and the results achieved.

That is often where companies find the talent they had not thought of hiring.
