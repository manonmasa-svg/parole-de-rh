Between recruitment difficulties, changing skills and the arrival of artificial intelligence, the French events industry is undergoing a profound transformation. For HR departments, professional training is becoming a strategic tool for recruitment, retention and competitiveness.

## A dynamic sector facing a skills shortage

The events industry plays an important role in the French economy. Trade shows, conferences, corporate conventions, cultural events and business meetings contribute to regional development, job creation and the visibility of companies.

According to the Event Data Book 2026 published by UNIMEV, the industry generates 10.4 billion euros in revenue based on consolidated 2023 data.

Yet behind this economic momentum, one difficulty persists: recruiting the professionals able to support this activity.

The study published in June 2026 by the joint observatory for digital, engineering, research and consulting jobs and event jobs (OPIIEC) shows that 54% of the HR managers surveyed have difficulty recruiting, particularly for technical and sales roles.

This finding illustrates a paradox: while companies need talent, they find it hard to identify candidates who are immediately operational.

The question is therefore no longer just finding employees, but also developing the skills available.

## Jobs that demand growing versatility

Organising an event is no longer limited to logistics coordination.

Professionals must now master several dimensions: project management, business development, digital communication, technology tools, supplier coordination, budget monitoring and the participant experience.

To these skills are added essential soft skills: adaptability, stress management, communication, negotiation and quick resolution of unexpected problems.

This versatility changes recruitment criteria.

A candidate with solid sales experience can, after suitable training, move into organising trade shows. Likewise, a professional with a background in communication, tourism or project management often has skills that transfer to event jobs.

For recruiters, this approach opens up interesting prospects: rather than looking exclusively for profiles with identical sector experience, it becomes possible to identify aptitudes and build the complementary skills.

## Career change, a still under-used talent pool

Career change is one possible answer to recruitment pressures.

Many working people today are looking for a job more focused on human contact, organisation, creativity or project management.

The events industry can meet these aspirations, provided the realities of the sector are presented clearly: working-hour constraints, travel, budget responsibility and managing many contacts at once.

A successful career change therefore relies on practical preparation.

Vocational training courses make it possible in particular to acquire the fundamentals of the job, to understand the economics of an event and to become familiar with the tools used by companies.

Role plays, case studies and practical exercises are particularly useful to prepare future employees for operational realities.

For an HR department, hiring a person changing careers can thus become a relevant approach, as long as their previous skills are recognised and their onboarding is structured.

## Artificial intelligence is speeding up the transformation of jobs

The digital revolution adds a new dimension to training needs.

According to OPIIEC, 53% of the companies surveyed plan to adopt solutions using artificial intelligence.

The potential applications are many: participant data analysis, content preparation, administrative automation, audience segmentation, personalised communications and optimisation of certain management tasks.

Artificial intelligence, however, replaces neither human relationships nor the ability to organise a complex event.

Above all, it changes the way certain tasks are carried out.

Professionals must learn to use these tools, check the results they produce, protect confidential information and preserve the quality of the experience offered to participants.

The challenge for employers is to support this change without creating a divide between employees already familiar with technology and those who are less so.

Continuing training is an essential lever here.

## Training rather than always looking for the ideal profile

Faced with recruitment difficulties, companies would do well to review their skills management strategy.

Waiting for the candidate with every qualification sought can prolong vacancies and weaken the organisation.

Another approach is to recruit on the basis of aptitude, then complete the know-how with targeted training.

This approach can take several forms: onboarding pathways, mentoring, short courses, individual coaching or internal skills development.

It also requires close collaboration between HR managers and operational managers to identify real needs.

Training then becomes an investment in the company's production capacity, but also in its social stability.

An employee who has career prospects can more easily picture a future in the organisation.

## Retention, as important as recruitment

The appeal of jobs does not depend solely on how interesting the tasks are.

It also rests on working conditions, recognition, career prospects and the quality of management.

In the events industry, periods of intense activity and atypical hours call for particular attention to how work is organised.

Manager training is therefore a major issue.

Knowing how to share out responsibilities, anticipate periods of overload, communicate effectively and support teams helps to reduce internal tensions.

Companies that combine skills development with better management practices can strengthen their ability to retain their employees.

## Making training a real HR strategy

The changes in the events industry illustrate a broader shift in the labour market.

In many sectors, skills are changing faster than traditional career paths.

Companies can therefore no longer see training as a simple one-off response to an administrative obligation.

It must be part of a talent development policy that combines recruitment, internal mobility, career change and anticipation of technological change.

To measure its effectiveness, HR departments can track several indicators: time to hire, autonomy of new employees, career mobility, retention and team satisfaction.

The goal is not to multiply training courses, but to develop the skills that are genuinely useful to the business.

In an industry where the human experience remains essential, companies' success will depend as much on their ability to attract new talent as on their ability to develop the people they already employ.

Professional training is no longer just a tool for adapting. It is becoming a real lever for sustainable growth.
